01 — Where to Live

Nasr City is Where You Want to Be

If you are coming to Egypt to study Arabic, Quran, or the Islamic sciences, Nasr City (مدينة نصر — Madinat Nasr) is almost certainly where you should live. It is the unofficial hub of the foreign Muslim student community in Cairo. Nearly every major institute — Fajr Center, Al-Ibaanah, Lisanul Arab, Ma'sarawi, and others — is located within walking distance of each other. You will find other students on your doorstep, which matters more than most people expect when you first arrive.

The best districts within Nasr City for students are the 8th, 9th, and 10th districts (Hayy Thamin, Tasi', and 'Aashir). These are the closest to the institutes and where most foreign students live. If a Cairo taxi driver does not know the street name, tell them "Souq As-Sayarat in Nasr City" — this large Friday car market is a landmark almost every driver knows.

03 — How to Find a Flat

Where Students Actually Find Housing

There is no central listing site for student housing in Egypt. Most flats are found through one of these channels — listed in order of what students report working best:

  • WhatsApp groups — your most important resource. Once connected to other students or an institute, ask to be added to student WhatsApp groups. Flats come up here constantly and are often already vetted by someone who knows the area.
  • Ask at your institute on arrival. Many institutes have informal notice boards or staff who know of available flats. One of the easiest routes if you arrive before finding somewhere.
  • Facebook groups. Search "Egypt expats," "Cairo housing," "Muslims in Cairo." People post available rooms regularly. Check post dates — listings go stale quickly.
  • Local brokers (simsars). Estate agents in Nasr City can find you something quickly but charge a fee — usually one month's rent. Only use one if you are pressed for time and willing to pay. Make sure any broker comes recommended by another student.
  • Ask other students directly. If you know anyone who has been to Egypt, message them. Someone in your community almost certainly knows of a spare room or can add you to the right WhatsApp group.
Important

Do not send money for a flat before you have physically seen it. Scams targeting foreign students exist. Always view the property in person before paying any deposit.

04 — What to Watch Out For

Common Housing Problems (and How to Avoid Them)

  • Overpaying because you arrived without a local contact Landlords charge more when they can see you are a new arrival with no reference point. Before agreeing to a price, ask another student what is normal for the area and flat size.
  • Electricity bills becoming unexpectedly high in summer Air conditioning in the Egyptian summer consumes a lot of electricity. If bills are not included in your rent, budget extra from March onwards. Always ask the landlord what the average monthly bill looks like.
  • Landlords being unresponsive to repairs It is common for landlords in Egypt to be slow when something breaks. Inspect the flat thoroughly before committing — check water pressure, all taps, electrical sockets, and any appliances included.
  • Tap water Do not drink the tap water in Cairo. It is not safe to consume directly. Buy bottled water or install a filter. Budget for this — it is an ongoing cost most guides do not mention.
  • Contracts in Arabic only If your landlord produces a rental contract, it will likely be in Arabic. Do not sign anything you cannot read without having someone trustworthy translate it first. A student who has been there longer or your institute may be able to help.
05 — Before You Commit

Questions to Ask Before Signing Anything

Whether you are messaging a landlord from abroad or viewing a flat in person, make sure you have clear answers to the following before you agree to anything:

  • Is electricity included in the monthly price? What about water and internet?
  • How much is the deposit, and under what conditions do I get it back?
  • Is there a written contract? Can I get a translated copy?
  • Who do I contact if something breaks?
  • Does the flat have a water filter or will I need to buy bottled water?
  • Is there air conditioning? Who pays for the electricity it uses?
  • How far is the flat from the nearest institute?
  • Are there other Muslim students or foreign students in the building?
  • Is there a washing machine, or is there a laundry nearby?
06 — Once You Arrive

Getting Settled in the First Week

Most experienced students recommend booking short-term accommodation for your first few days — even a cheap hotel or hostel in Nasr City — so you have a base while you look around in person. Trying to commit to a flat from abroad without seeing it is a common mistake.

First week priorities

Get a local SIM card on arrival (Vodafone Egypt, Orange, and Etisalat are the main providers). Set up Uber or InDrive for transport. Ask at your institute to be added to the student WhatsApp groups. Everything else becomes easier once you are in those groups.

For money, use a Starling or Revolut card — both work well in Egypt with no foreign transaction fees. There are exchange offices throughout Nasr City where you can get a good rate on cash. Avoid airport exchange bureaus, which offer significantly worse rates.
